Beyond Erdős Problem 193

Only basis steps.
How few points on a line?

Take an infinite walk on the integer lattice, moving one unit forward along one coordinate axis at each step. What is the smallest possible bound on the number of vertices lying on any straight line?

Erdős Problem 193 ↗ asks for a 3D lattice walk with a finite set of allowed steps and no three collinear vertices. Here the step rule is stricter: only the positive standard basis vectors +e0, …, +ed−1 are allowed. Allowing more dimensions leads to stronger known avoidance bounds under this restriction.

A companion to Brown-Gerver-Ramsey Theorems in Small Dimensions
by Stijn Cambie, Erik Kalviainen, and Jeffrey Shallit.

How does a higher-dimensional walk fit on this screen?
  1. Start in the original lattice. P = (x0, …, xd−1) records the number of steps in each direction. Their sum is the vertex index.
  2. Map to three display coordinates. Each direction ej gets a 3D vector vj, listed below. The displayed point is Q = x0v0 + ⋯ + xd−1vd−1. This is the identity in 3D, the manuscript’s return-displacement map in 4D/5D, and an illustrative linear map in 6D.
  3. Optionally compress the overall direction. For the complete selected prefix, take the unit vector u from Q0 to QN. With compression factor c, use Q′ = Q − (1 − 1/c)(Q · u)u. Only the component parallel to u is shortened. At c = 1, nothing is compressed; this is not a different walk.
  4. Rotate, then draw on a flat screen. The initial camera is chosen to show the broad face of the selected point cloud rather than an edge-on line. You can rotate it freely. We take two screen coordinates, with one common scale to fit the canvas.

02 / The idea

From letter counts to collinearity.

The construction in two formulas

Choose a word w with letters 0, …, d − 1. Each letter selects one positive unit step:

P0=0,Pn=∑i=0n−1ewi

For a < b < c, the three vertices lie on one line exactly when the two intervening blocks have equal step proportions:

Pb−Pab−a=Pc−Pbc−b

The strategy: construct the word so equal proportions cannot persist across too many consecutive blocks. For k collinear vertices, all k − 1 block ratios must agree—even if the blocks have different lengths.

1

Words become walks

Read a letter j, take a step ej. Each vertex is a prefix’s letter-count vector. Consecutive blocks with identical letter proportions make collinear boundary vertices, even when their lengths differ.

2

Descend through digits

The constructions repeat at base-2 or base-3 scales. Equal-state chords obey an exact valuation identity: the power of 2 or 3 dividing a quadratic expression matches the power dividing the index difference.

3

Too few residues

Collinearity would force pairwise gaps to have the same valuation. Three indices cannot do this in base 2, nor four in base 3. The auxiliary geometry turns that contradiction into the lattice bounds.

How the three results fit together

6D: square-corner state tags force any hypothetical collinear triple into one state. The Gaussian norm and 2-adic identity rule it out. Six auxiliary step types become six standard basis directions.

4D: take returns to one state in the ternary construction. Their four displacement types give a four-letter walk. The quadratic form X² − 3Y² and a modulo-3 contradiction exclude four collinear vertices.

3D: transform those four return steps into two coordinate steps and two diagonal steps. Subdivide the diagonals. The vertices lie in a set S and its translate S + e₂, each meeting any line at most three times. Together, at most six.

Still open in this snapshot: no-three-collinear positive-basis walks in 4D or 5D, and no-four-collinear in 3D. The related original Erdős 193 theorem allows a different, arbitrary finite step set.
